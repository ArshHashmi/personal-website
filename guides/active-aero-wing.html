How-to guide

Build an active aero wing

A rear wing that reads how hard the car is accelerating or braking and moves itself to suit: flat under throttle, a shallow angle at cruise, fully up as an airbrake when you slow down. No driver input, and nothing wired into the car. This is how mine was built, with the reasoning behind each decision so you can change what you need to.

What you are building.

A fixed wing is a compromise. Set it up for grip through a corner and it holds you back on the straight; set it up for a low-drag top speed and it gives you very little when you need the rear planted. This one does not pick. An accelerometer on the wing reads longitudinal acceleration and drives a servo to one of three angles.

Neutral−5°

Under throttle. The wing lies almost flat so it is barely fighting the air. The least drag of the three.

88.4 N downforce · 9.4 N drag
Cruise+5°

Holding a steady speed. A small angle buys real grip for almost no drag. The efficient one, and where the car spends most of its life.

882.4 N downforce · 10.3 N drag
Airbrake+40°

Under braking. The wing stands up into the air and becomes a brake: downforce to plant the car, drag to help slow it.

1,708.8 N downforce · 262.0 N drag

Those figures are from CFD at a 30 mph inlet, so treat them as the shape of the behaviour rather than what your car will feel. The useful comparison is between them: going from neutral to cruise multiplies downforce roughly tenfold for under a newton of extra drag, and that is the whole argument for a cambered section that works at a shallow angle instead of a flat plate you have to tip into the wind.

Before you start.

None of this is difficult on its own. It is a printed part, a hobby servo and about forty lines of Arduino. The work is in getting the geometry right and being honest about the mounting.

  • CAD you can loft in. Fusion 360 was used here. Any parametric package works.
  • An FDM printer. Anything with a bed around 200 mm. The wing is printed in sections precisely so the bed size stops mattering.
  • Soldering, at a basic level. A few dupont joints and a barrel jack.
  • Enough Arduino to upload a sketch and read the serial monitor. The firmware is given in full below.
Read this part before you build the mount

Mine ran on suction cups, which is the right call for testing because it leaves the car untouched and comes off in seconds. It is not the right call for sustained speed. A wing of this size makes real force, and the report's own conclusion is that a future version should fasten to the trunk lid or lip properly. Decide how it is held on before you decide how fast to go with it, keep early testing to low speeds on a road you know, and check the mount every single time.

Tools: a soldering iron (it does double duty for both the electrical work and for plastic-welding the wing seams), a heat-set insert tip, hex keys, and a laptop for the serial monitor during calibration.

Step 1. Draw the section.

Everything downstream is set by the cross section, so this is the part worth slowing down for.

1
Start from a cambered profile, mounted inverted

Take a standard cambered airfoil and turn it upside down. Right way up it makes lift; inverted, the same pressure difference works downward and makes downforce.

Then modify it: more curvature on the upper surface so the flow accelerates over it and static pressure drops, and a flatter lower surface to hold a higher-pressure region underneath. That asymmetry is the downforce.

Why not a symmetric section?

A symmetric profile like a NACA 0012 makes nothing at zero angle of attack. Every newton of cruise downforce would have to be bought with servo angle, which means the actuator holds a load for most of the drive. A cambered inverted section makes downforce passively at neutral incidence, so the servo mostly rests.

2
Lay out the span in three pieces

1,400 mm tip to tip, split into a 900 mm centre section that holds the profile constant and does most of the work, and two 250 mm outboard sections either side.

The outboard pieces are lofted, not extruded: the inboard end matches the centre profile exactly, the outboard end is a higher-angle version of it. That puts more downforce toward the tips and spreads the load better across the span, and lofting between two sketches rather than butting two extrusions together means there is no hard geometric break for the flow to trip over.

3
Add endplates, and make them do two jobs

A wingtip leaks: high-pressure air from one surface curls around the end to the low-pressure side. That leak is induced drag, and it eats the effective lift coefficient. A vertical plate at each tip blocks the path.

While you are there, make them the mounting interface too. M3 heat-set inserts moulded into the endplate with clearance holes on the mating face means screws thread straight in and nothing needs a nut behind it. You will be taking this apart repeatedly during tuning, and that decision pays for itself quickly.

Step 2. Print it.

The original plan was XPS foam cut with a hot wire against printed templates, faired against a printed mould, then laminated in fibreglass and epoxy. It was abandoned before it started, and it is worth knowing why.

Hot-wire cutting is only as good as the hand guiding it, and any deviation propagates through every slice in the stack. Fairing foam down to a CAD-defined profile is slow with no guaranteed dimensional accuracy, and lamination adds its own variables on top. Printing gives you the CAD geometry every time, and features that would be miserable to build into a foam core — hinge bores, dowel holes, insert pockets, servo mounting geometry — are simply part of the part. You pay for that in mass. It is worth it.

4
Split the span into 200 mm modules

No consumer bed swallows 1,400 mm, and a single-piece wing would mean reprinting the whole thing to fix any one part of it. Cut the centre element into 200 mm sections and print them individually.

Low infill with three walls is deliberate. On a part like this the shell carries the load, so walls buy you stiffness where infill would only buy you mass in the middle where it does the least.

5
Join with dowels, then weld the seams

Bore a 10 mm dowel hole into each mating face and pin the sections together. The dowels do two jobs: they hold spanwise alignment so the profile stays continuous across the joint, and they carry shear across it so the wing behaves as one structural element instead of a row of separately loaded segments.

Then fuse the joints. Run a soldering iron tip along each external seam so the PLA melts and intermixes across the joint face. Dowels handle alignment and shear; the weld gives you continuous rigidity along the bond line so nothing can shift under load. Do this on the seams between the 200 mm centre modules and at the junction with the angled outboard sections.

Why bother with both

Dowels alone leave a pinned multi-body assembly that can move slightly under aerodynamic load. The weld turns it into one part. Mechanical alignment plus thermal joining is the standard answer for FDM assemblies that need both positional accuracy and structural continuity.

Step 3. Make it move.

The fewer parts between the servo shaft and the wing, the less slop there is to fight. This is a direct drive with nothing in between.

6
Pocket the servo into the bracket

Model a pocket in the support bracket that the DS3218 drops into flush, and fix it through its mounting ears with M3 screws into heat-set inserts in the bracket body. Line the servo output shaft up with the wing's hinge axis. If those two are coaxial there is no linkage to design.

7
Print your own servo horn

Do not use the horn in the box. Generic horns on third-party splines have angular play, and any play at the horn is multiplied out at the wing tip. Model a horn around the actual spline geometry of your servo so it goes on with zero slop.

Why direct drive instead of a pushrod

A pushrod linkage is easier to package, but a slender rod resisting the hinge moment is a buckling risk exactly when you least want one: in the airbrake position, at maximum aerodynamic load. Direct drive has nothing to buckle.

8
Mirror the second servo

There is one servo at each end, and they face opposite directions. They therefore need supplementary angles: whatever you send to one, send 180 − pos to the other, or they will fight each other across the hinge. The firmware below does this in a single function so you can never forget it.

Step 4. Wire it.

Five components and a shared ground. The only part with a trap in it is where the servo power comes from.

9
Power the servos from the buck, not from the Nano

A loaded DS3218 can pull over an amp while it moves. The Nano's onboard regulator is not rated for that, and it shares a pass element with the board's own logic supply, so a servo transient can brown out the microcontroller.

Feed both servos straight from the LM2596's 5 V output, bypassing the Nano's regulator entirely. The Nano and the sensor run from the same 5 V rail, with the MPU-6050 drawing from the Nano's regulated 5 V pin, which is fine because its draw is tiny. Everything shares a common ground: I²C will not work reliably without it, and neither will the servo signal reference.

10
Add the two capacitors

100 µF electrolytic across the buck output, for bulk storage that absorbs the current surge when a servo starts moving so the rail does not sag. 100 nF ceramic across the Nano's VIN and GND, for the high-frequency switching noise off the servo motors. The electrolytic's ESR is too high to catch that; the ceramic's is low enough.

The GY-521 breakout already carries its own decoupling, so the I²C lines need nothing added.

Use a barrel jack for the battery so the pack can come off without disturbing anything else. Route the loom through the bracket's own lattice and zip-tie it at intervals, so nothing moves under vibration.

Mount the IMU flat, and mind which way it faces

The sensor needs to sit as level as you can get it, with its X axis pointing along the direction of travel. Any tilt bleeds gravity into the longitudinal reading, which is exactly the signal the whole system runs on. Fasten it through its mounting holes with M3, then add tape or a zip tie: it has to hold position under vibration and aerodynamic buffeting, not just sit there.

Step 5. The firmware.

Three states, not a continuous curve. The wing lives at three defined angles, so a threshold comparison is enough and it is far more robust against sensor noise than a proportional loop would be. Everything interesting is what wraps around that comparison.

You need three libraries: Servo.h and Wire.h ship with the Arduino IDE, and MPU6050.h is the Electronic Cats library, installed through the Library Manager.

12
Read the sensor, and clean up the signal

Pull the raw 16-bit accelerometer value over I²C and divide by 16,384 to get gravities, which is the scale factor for the sensor's default ±2 g range. Then two corrections:

  • Subtract a fixed offset. Mine was 0.07 g, which is gravity bleeding into the longitudinal axis because the sensor is not mounted perfectly level. Yours will be different. You measure it in step 14.
  • Average the last ten samples. A rolling buffer, replacing the oldest reading each pass. Raw MEMS output in a car is full of engine vibration, road input and the wing's own buffeting, and without this you get threshold crossings that are noise rather than driving.
13
Compare, then wait a second before you believe it

Above +0.09 g go to neutral; below −0.07 g go to airbrake; anything between is cruise. The important part is what happens next: a new mode has to hold for a full second before it is executed.

The debounce is the whole trick

Without it the wing chases every pothole, gear change and throttle twitch, and earlier versions of this firmware hunted between positions constantly. A one-second gate on millis() costs you nothing you would notice while driving and removes the problem completely. The loop keeps polling at full rate during the wait, it just does not act.

The loop runs on a 50 ms delay, a 20 Hz update rate. That is far faster than it needs to be, since aerodynamic loads on a road car change over hundreds of milliseconds, but it costs nothing and the debounce is what actually sets the response time.

#include <Servo.h>
#include <Wire.h>
#include <MPU6050.h>

Servo servo1; Servo servo2;
MPU6050 mpu;

// Wing positions, in servo units. Calibrate these yourself (step 15).
#define POS_NEUTRAL   110   // under acceleration
#define POS_CRUISE     65   // steady speed
#define POS_AIRBRAKE    0   // under braking

// Thresholds, in g, applied after the offset below
#define ACCEL_THRESHOLD  0.09
#define BRAKE_THRESHOLD -0.07

// Your mounting offset. Measure it, do not copy mine.
#define OFFSET         0.07
#define FILTER_SIZE      10   // rolling average window
#define CHANGE_DELAY   1000   // debounce, ms

float readings[FILTER_SIZE];
int filterIndex = 0;
float filterSum = 0;
unsigned long lastChangeTime = 0;
int currentPosition = POS_CRUISE;
int targetPosition  = POS_CRUISE;

void setWingPosition(int pos) {
  // The servos are mirrored across the hinge, so they take
  // supplementary angles or they fight each other.
  servo1.write(pos);
  servo2.write(180 - pos);
  currentPosition = pos;
}

float getFilteredAccel() {
  int16_t ax, ay, az, gx, gy, gz;
  mpu.getMotion6(&ax, &ay, &az, &gx, &gy, &gz);
  float accelG = (ax / 16384.0) - OFFSET;
  filterSum -= readings[filterIndex];
  readings[filterIndex] = accelG;
  filterSum += readings[filterIndex];
  filterIndex = (filterIndex + 1) % FILTER_SIZE;
  return filterSum / FILTER_SIZE;
}

void setup() {
  servo1.attach(5);
  servo2.attach(6);
  Serial.begin(9600);
  Wire.begin();
  mpu.initialize();
  Serial.println(mpu.testConnection() ? "MPU6050 connected"
                                      : "MPU6050 connection failed");
  for (int i = 0; i < FILTER_SIZE; i++) readings[i] = 0;
  setWingPosition(POS_CRUISE);
  Serial.println("Wing starting at cruise position");
}

void loop() {
  float accel = getFilteredAccel();
  Serial.print("Accel: "); Serial.print(accel); Serial.print("g -> ");

  if (accel > ACCEL_THRESHOLD) {
    Serial.println("NEUTRAL (accelerating)");
    targetPosition = POS_NEUTRAL;
  } else if (accel < BRAKE_THRESHOLD) {
    Serial.println("AIRBRAKE (braking)");
    targetPosition = POS_AIRBRAKE;
  } else {
    Serial.println("CRUISE");
    targetPosition = POS_CRUISE;
  }

  // Only commit once the new mode has held for CHANGE_DELAY.
  if (targetPosition != currentPosition) {
    if (millis() - lastChangeTime >= CHANGE_DELAY) {
      setWingPosition(targetPosition);
      lastChangeTime = millis();
    }
  } else {
    lastChangeTime = millis();
  }

  delay(50);   // 20 Hz
}
Do not trust my position numbers

The three POS_ values are servo units, not degrees, and they were found empirically on my hardware. Your servo horn will be clocked differently and your hinge geometry will differ, so those numbers will put your wing somewhere else entirely. Step 15 is how you find your own.

Step 6. Bench test, before it goes near the car.

Everything here is checkable on a desk with the Nano on USB and the 12 V pack feeding the buck. Do all of it.

14
Work through it in order
  • I²C scan first. Confirm the MPU-6050 answers at 0x68. If it does not, nothing after this matters.
  • Each servo alone, then both together. Confirms D5 and D6 are wired right and that the mirrored logic actually turns them the same way at the hinge.
  • Find your offset. Sit the sensor still and level and watch the serial output. Whatever it reads instead of zero is your OFFSET. Mine was 0.07 g.
  • Confirm the axis. Tilt the sensor along each axis in turn and watch which one responds. That is the one that must point along the car.
  • Prove the filter works. Tap the sensor sharply. The raw value should jump and the filtered value should barely move. If a tap crosses a threshold, your filter is not doing its job.
  • Hand-tilt through all three modes and confirm the wing goes where it should, and holds position when you push against it.
15
Calibrate your own position values

Sweep the servos through their range using serial input and note the servo value at each of the three physical angles you want: −5° for neutral, +5° for cruise, +40° for the airbrake. Those three numbers are your POS_NEUTRAL, POS_CRUISE and POS_AIRBRAKE.

The full sweep is 45°, comfortably inside the DS3218's 270° range, so the servo stays in its strongest region throughout.

Step 7. On the car.

Mine went on the trunk lid of a 2011 BMW E93. Nothing about the design is specific to that car, which was the point.

16
Position the brackets

Mount the two brackets symmetrically about the car's centreline so the aerodynamic load is shared evenly, and put the wing at the rearmost practical point on the lid. The further back it sits, the longer the moment arm to the rear axle and the more of its downforce ends up where you want it.

17
Tune it on a road, not on the bench

Run a wire from the Nano into the cabin and keep a laptop on the serial monitor in the passenger seat. You are watching two things: the live acceleration reading, and whether the mode changes land where the driving actually changes.

This is how the 0.07 g offset was found and how both thresholds were settled — under real road conditions rather than by guessing on a desk. A camera pointed at the wing is worth setting up too: it gives you a record you can replay against the serial log to check that transitions happened where you thought they did.

What good looks like

The wing should flatten under normal throttle, sit at cruise at steady speed, and deploy under normal braking — all without you having to drive unusually hard to trigger it. It should also sit still at a constant cruise rather than hunting between modes. If it hunts, your thresholds are too close together or your filter window is too short.

What broke, and what to do about it.

One failure, and it is the most useful thing in this guide because you can design it out before you print anything.

The connecting arm between the servo horn and the wing fractured during testing, at one of its thinner sections. The wall thickness there was not enough to carry the bending moment from the horn while the aerodynamic hinge moment was working on the wing at the same time. The system kept running afterwards, but with degraded positional accuracy on that side.

Three changes to make before you print yours

More wall thickness at the narrow point of the connecting arm. A larger fillet radius where it changes section, since a sharp internal corner is a stress concentration and that is where it let go. And CF-PLA for that one part, which buys stiffness for very little mass. This is a part-level mistake with a part-level fix; nothing about the architecture needs to change.

Where you could take it.

The architecture was drawn with more than one axis in mind, which is why the IMU's full six degrees of freedom are read even though the controller uses exactly one of them.

  • A lateral axis. The lateral acceleration data is already there. A second servo axis on the hinge would let the wing tilt through a corner to generate a yaw-stabilising moment.
  • Telemetry. Moving to an ESP32 buys wireless logging of wing angle, acceleration and mode state to a phone, which turns threshold tuning into something driven by data rather than by feel.
  • A proper mount. Fastening to the trunk lid or lip instead of suction cups, which also lets you set the wing at a better height above the surface.
